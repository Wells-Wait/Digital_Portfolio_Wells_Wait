FSAE

Formula Society of Automotive Engineers (FSAE) is a club on campus that builds a car and competes. I joined freshman year on the software subsystem to ensure my engineering skills stay diverse. After all, I am interested in the connection of software and mechanical systems and what is a better example than a car. Well, that might be an EV car.

This is my second year in the club and the first year they will be building an EV car in addition to the IC car. And most of my projects this year are on this brand new style of car. The below three sections go over the Screen, Vehicle Control Unit (VCU), and Battery Management System (BMS).

Screen

The screen is the main interface for both the IC and EV car displaying the state of both cars. The screen system comprises two sections: rev-lights and of course a screen. The rev-lights are the most important part for the drivers as they are fast to read and indicate shifting, warnings, and on the EV, derate. There is one screen per driver as well as screens for diagnostics so each subsystem has the information they need during testing.

The screen runs on a Pi using Qt to handle the graphics. The screen gets car data through shared memory Protobuf packets. The shared memory was developed by me to help connect the screen, VCU, and the rest of the systems on the Pi. The screen collects the data in C++, checks warnings, and displays everything to the driver.

Battery Monitoring System

The Battery Monitoring System (BMS) is built to watch the battery on the new EV car and ensure safety. Its primary goal is ensuring the battery does not overheat, but it sends data over CAN to other systems. This data is used to ensure the car never reaches a point where the BMS must trigger a shutdown. In addition to safety, the BMS also does cell balancing. For this year's BMS, the cell balancing is only done through discharge. This means it is only really used during charging.

The battery is broken into 5 modules, each with 24 groups, with each group having 4 cells. The BMS measures the voltage of all 120 groups and ensures that their voltages are all good. In addition, the BMS measures 100 temperatures across the battery to ensure safety of each module. The battery has a master and 10 slave boards with communication done through daisy chain ISO-SPI to ensure safety and quality signals. These signals are then checked for safety, and two packets are sent over CAN to the rest of the car. One is high-speed safety and warning data while the second is a low-speed multiplexed packet for later analysis of all 225 battery data points as well as current and a few more things.

Vehicle Control System

The Vehicle Control System (VCU) controls the EV car, much like the name indicates. While it sounds cool, its primary goal like most systems is safety. It takes in data from around the car and if safety is compromised, shuts down the EV car. Unlike something like the BMS, however, the VCU also works to avoid shutdowns through power derate. If the car is indicating minor issues, it will decrease the torque to the motor to lower thermal or other issues. The VCU, like the screen, is run on the Pi of the EV and also uses shared memory Protobuf for communication. The VCU is safety-focused, but it is a control system and handles the inverter control, cooling, and providing some data to the driver through a link with the screen.

This is the first year with an EV car, so this, like the BMS, is an entirely new system. This means it is basic this year, but in the future, the VCU will be handling slip, multi-motor control, and more. Right now it is critical and has many features, but the primary challenge is getting an EV car running with little experience.
